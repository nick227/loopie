# Internal Training: Client Communication & Approvals

## Objective
Make working with us feel easy while preserving clear client control.

## Core Rule
**We recommend. The client approves. We execute. We report.**

Do not make the client manage implementation details unless necessary.

## What Requires Approval
Typically:
- new messaging concepts,
- new offers,
- new customer-facing templates,
- major audience changes,
- new recurring outreach,
- new automation behavior,
- changes in brand tone,
- promotions,
- significant process changes.

Routine execution of an already approved process does not need repeated approval.

## Approve the Playbook, Not Every Instance
Example:

Instead of asking:
“Can we send this follow-up to Jane?”

Get approval for:
“When a new lead does not reply within 3 days, send this approved follow-up.”

Once approved, the system can apply that rule consistently.

## How to Present Recommendations
Keep approval requests short.

Include:
- what we want to do,
- who it affects,
- the exact content,
- when it will happen,
- what happens afterward,
- the expected benefit.

Avoid dumping technical configuration on the client.

## Example Approval Format

### Proposal
Send a re-engagement email to customers who have not purchased in 6 months.

### Audience
127 past customers.

### Message
Use the approved “We Miss You” template with the current offer.

### Follow-Up
If there is no reply after 5 days, send one reminder.

### Action Needed
Approve, request changes, or decline.

## Client Feedback
When a client requests a change:
1. Clarify the requested outcome.
2. Update the content or process.
3. Record the decision.
4. Confirm the revised version.
5. Do not silently reinterpret approval.

## Avoid
- sending first and asking later,
- vague approval language,
- excessive technical detail,
- pushing clients into strategies they do not understand,
- hiding poor performance,
- presenting automation as uncontrollable.

## Reporting Language
Reports should answer:
- What did we do?
- What happened?
- What needs attention?
- What do we recommend next?

Prefer business language:
“12 customers replied.”

Avoid unnecessary technical language:
“12 conversion events triggered the reply-state workflow.”

## Difficult Situations
If something underperforms:
- state what happened,
- show the relevant data,
- explain the likely cause,
- recommend the next action.

If we make a mistake:
- identify the issue clearly,
- stop further impact,
- correct what can be corrected,
- document what changed to prevent recurrence.

## Long-Term Goal
As the software improves, clients should need less direct help from us.

Our training, templates, defaults, and automation should gradually transfer routine work from our team to the product.

That transition must never reduce clarity or client control.
